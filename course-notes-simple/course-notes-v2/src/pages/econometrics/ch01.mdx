## What is Econometrics?

**Econometrics** = the use of statistical methods to analyze economic data.

Econometricians typically work with **non-experimental data** (observational data, not data from controlled lab experiments).

### Typical Goals of Econometric Analysis

| Goal | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Estimation** | Estimating relationships between economic variables |
| **Testing** | Testing economic theories and hypotheses |
| **Forecasting** | Predicting future economic variables |
| **Policy Evaluation** | Evaluating government and business policies |

---

## Steps in Econometric Analysis

> **The Two-Step Process:**
> 1. **Economic Model** — Often based on theory (utility maximization, equilibrium, etc.). This step is sometimes skipped in practice.
> 2. **Econometric Model** — Translate the economic model into a form that can be estimated with data. This involves specifying functional forms and identifying measurable variables.

### Economic Models

Economic models can be:
- **Micro-models** (individual/firm behavior)
- **Macro-models** (economy-wide behavior)

They often use optimizing behavior, equilibrium modeling, and establishing relationships between variables.

**Examples:** Demand equations, pricing equations, production functions.

---

## Example 1: Economic Model of Crime (Becker, 1968)

Based on **utility maximization**, the model suggests criminal activity depends on:

| Factor | Effect on Crime |
|--------|-----------------|
| "Wage" of criminal activities | ↑ Higher illegal wage → more crime |
| Wage for legal employment | ↑ Higher legal wage → less crime |
| Other income | ↑ More outside income → less crime |
| Probability of getting caught | ↑ Higher probability → less crime |
| Probability of conviction | ↑ Higher conviction rate → less crime |
| Expected sentence length | ↑ Longer sentence → less crime |
| Age | Varies by life stage |

> **Note:** The functional form of the relationship is **not specified** in the pure economic model. The equation could have been postulated without any formal economic modeling.

## Example 2: Model of Job Training & Productivity

**Question:** What is the effect of additional training on worker productivity?

A simple model:

```
Hourly wage = f(Years of education, Years of experience, Weeks of job training)
```

**Other factors** may also be relevant (innate ability, quality of education, family background), but the three variables above are considered the most important.

---

## From Economic Model to Econometric Model

### Econometric Model of Criminal Activity

The econometric version requires:

1. **Specifying the functional form** (e.g., linear, log-linear)
2. **Approximating variables** with measurable quantities:
   - "Measure of criminal activity" → arrest frequency, conviction rate
   - "Wage in criminal activity" → often unobserved, must be proxied

```
Crime = β₀ + β₁(Legal Wage) + β₂(Other Income) + β₃(Prior Arrests) 
      + β₄(Conviction Freq) + β₅(Avg Sentence) + β₆(Age) + u
```

Where **u** = unobserved determinants of criminal activity (moral character, family background, etc.)

### Econometric Model of Job Training

```
Wage = β₀ + β₁(Education) + β₂(Experience) + β₃(Training) + u
```

Where **u** = unobserved determinants of wage (innate ability, quality of education, etc.)

> **The Error Term (u):** Most of econometrics deals with the specification and properties of the **error term**. It captures all factors that affect the dependent variable but are not included in the model.

### Using Econometric Models for Hypothesis Testing

- The parameter **β₃** in the training equation represents the **"effect of training on wage"**
- Key questions: How large is this effect? Is it **statistically different from zero**?

## Types of Economic Data

Econometric methods depend heavily on the **nature of the data**. Using inappropriate methods leads to misleading results.

### 1. Cross-Sectional Data

- Sample of individuals, households, firms, cities, states, or countries at a **given point in time**
- Observations are more or less **independent** (pure random sampling)
- Sometimes violated: non-response, clustering
- Common in **applied microeconomics**

> **Example:** Data on wages and worker characteristics (education, experience) for 526 individuals at one point in time.

### 2. Time Series Data

- Observations of one or more variables **over time**
- Examples: stock prices, CPI, GDP, homicide rates, auto sales
- Observations are typically **serially correlated** (past values relate to future values)
- Features: **trends** and **seasonality**
- Common in **macroeconomics and finance**

> **Example:** Annual data on minimum wages, unemployment rates, and GNP from 1950–1987.

### 3. Pooled Cross Sections

- Two or more cross sections **combined** into one data set
- Cross sections are drawn **independently** of each other
- Often used to **evaluate policy changes** (before/after comparisons)

> **Example:** Random sample of house prices in 1993 (before tax reform) combined with a new random sample in 1995 (after reform) to measure the effect of property tax changes.

### 4. Panel (Longitudinal) Data

- The **same cross-sectional units** are followed over time
- Has both a **cross-sectional** and a **time series** dimension
- Advantages:
  - Can account for **time-invariant unobservables**
  - Can model **lagged responses**

> **Example:** City crime statistics where each city is observed in 1986 and 1990. This allows controlling for unobserved city characteristics that don't change over time.

### Summary Table: Data Types

| Data Type | Structure | Key Feature | Typical Use |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| **Cross-sectional** | Many units, one time period | Independent observations | Microeconomics |
| **Time series** | One unit, many time periods | Serial correlation | Macroeconomics, Finance |
| **Pooled cross sections** | Many units, a few time periods | Independent samples per period | Policy evaluation |
| **Panel/Longitudinal** | Same units, many time periods | Controls for unobservables | Advanced micro/macro |

## Causality and Ceteris Paribus

### The Core Problem

Most economic questions are **ceteris paribus** questions — "all else being equal."

**Definition:** The causal effect of X on Y is:
> *"How does Y change if X is changed, but all other relevant factors are held constant?"*

### Example 1: Fertilizer and Crop Yield

**Question:** By how much will soybean production increase if fertilizer is increased?

**Implicit assumption:** Quality of land, rainfall, parasites, etc. are held fixed.

**Ideal experiment:** Randomly assign different fertilizer amounts to plots. This works because fertilizer is **unrelated** to other yield factors.

### Example 2: Return to Education

**Question:** If a person receives one more year of education, how much does their wage increase?

**Problem:** We cannot run this experiment. Education is **related** to other wage factors (intelligence, family background). People with more education may differ in unobserved ways.

### Example 3: Police and Crime

**Question:** If a city gets 10 more police officers, how much does crime fall?

**Problem:** In reality, the number of police is **determined by** the crime rate (simultaneous causation). High-crime cities hire more police.

### Example 4: Minimum Wage and Unemployment

**Question:** By how much will unemployment increase if the minimum wage rises?

**Problem:** The minimum wage level depends on political and economic factors that also influence unemployment.

> **The Fundamental Challenge:** Without **random assignment**, the causal effect of X on Y is difficult to isolate because X is usually correlated with other factors that affect Y. This is the central problem econometric methods try to solve.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Econometrics** | Use of statistical methods to analyze economic data |
| **Economic model** | Theoretical relationship derived from economic principles |
| **Econometric model** | Empirical version of an economic model, ready for estimation |
| **Error term (u)** | Unobserved factors affecting the dependent variable |
| **Cross-sectional data** | Data on many units at a single point in time |
| **Time series data** | Data on one unit over multiple time periods |
| **Pooled cross sections** | Multiple independent cross sections combined |
| **Panel data** | Same units observed over multiple time periods |
| **Ceteris paribus** | "All other things being equal" |
| **Causal effect** | Change in Y resulting from a change in X, holding other factors constant |
| **OLS** | Ordinary Least Squares — the basic estimation method |
| **Heteroskedasticity** | Non-constant variance of the error term |
